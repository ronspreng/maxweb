I Was Drinking Five Coffees a Day to Stay Sharp — Why I Finally Quit at 64

A retired electrician explains how his coffee habit was masking the real issue with his afternoon focus — and what he found when he went looking for an alternative without stimulants.

Forty years as an electrician teaches you to respect the details. Wire colors, breaker ratings, torque specs — get one detail wrong and somebody has a very bad day. My memory for details was my livelihood. And my fuel was coffee. Two cups before work, one at ten, one after lunch, one around three. For decades, it worked.

I'm Tom, 64, from Ohio, retired three years now. Somewhere after my 60th birthday, the math stopped working. The coffee still made me feel busy — jittery, restless, up twice a night — but it stopped making me feel sharp. I'd walk to the garage and forget what I came for. I'd lose the thread of a story halfway through telling it. At my grandson's hockey game, I blanked on the name of a man I'd worked beside for fifteen years.

More Coffee Wasn't the Answer

My first instinct was, of course, a sixth cup. My wife put a stop to that — mostly because I was pacing the kitchen at 11 p.m. That's when it dawned on me: caffeine was covering up the problem, not addressing it. I felt alert, but the focus and recall I actually wanted weren't coming back with it.

My daughter-in-law works at a pharmacy, and she explained it in a way that stuck with me: a stimulant revs the engine, but it doesn't do anything for the wiring. As we get older, she said, research suggests our brains can become less efficient at using the nutrients that support memory and clear thinking. You can't caffeinate your way around that.
